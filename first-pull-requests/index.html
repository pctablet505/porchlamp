Porchlamp report: first pull requests · data to 24 October 2026

In most open source projects we measured, one person gives most of the first answers. Newcomers get merged more often there.

Porchlamp followed 95,078 first pull requests, each from someone who hadn't sent that project a pull request in about six months or more. Projects that lean on one person answer them within a day at least as often as projects that share the job.

Where newcomers get merged

More first pull requests get merged where one person does the answering

The busiest person's share of first answers

Stars and volume don't explain the merges. Comparing only projects with similar stars and a similar number of first pull requests, the gap is 13 percentage points (95% range 8 to 18). Leaving out projects owned by the busiest person's own account, and projects where only one person answers at all, leaves 140 and 92 projects and a gap of 13 percentage points (95% range 7 to 19). The same two comparisons in answers give 6 points (1 to 12) and 4 points (−3 to 11).

The typical project is the median of 1,199 projects with at least 20 first answers we could attribute to a person and at least 10 first pull requests at least 14 days old: 702 with one person at 90% or more, 93 with nobody over half. Each project counts once, so a busy one weighs no more than a small one, and a project has to answer often to clear that floor. That's why these shares run higher than the page's counts by pull request: 47% answered within two weeks, 32% merged. Another 961 projects have at least 10 first pull requests and fewer than 20 such answers: the typical one answers 23% of its first pull requests within two weeks. In 572 of them one person gave nearly all of the few answers, and the typical one answers 30%.

One possible reason is the bystander effect: a job that's clearly one person's gets done, and a job that's everyone's can wait. This compares projects, so it can't prove that. Shared projects may also get harder pull requests or have stricter rules for merging.

How long to wait

After two weeks of silence, the odds of a merge have more than halved

More than half of first pull requests, 53%, hear nothing from the project's people for two weeks, and the longer the silence, the lower the odds of a merge. Each point counts pull requests at least four months old that had no answer by that day, and how many of them were merged in the end: 17% after a week of silence, 13% after two weeks, 9% after a month, 5% after two months. By then the two lines nearly meet, at 5% and 6%.

A late answer is still possible. Of first pull requests silent at two weeks, 11% hear something by day 30, 21% by day 60 and 27% by day 90.

What helps

Authors who asked got about 9 percentage points more answers. A comment with no ask and no tag got none.

Of first pull requests silent at two weeks, 28% got an answer in the next 30 days (by day 44) if the author had asked in the past week, with words like "friendly ping" or "could you take a look". In the same projects, with no comment after the first hour, it was 14%. Part of that gap comes from which projects get asked. Inside one project, about 9 percentage points remain. A comment with no ask and no tag shows nothing.

How this was measured

  • Data. Porchlamp's 2026-11-05 snapshot holds 1,726,945 outside pull requests opened from 27 April 2026 to 24 October 2026, across 10,998 projects with a record. These results read 592,986 of them, those with recorded replies after the rules below.
  • Left out. Bots, drafts never marked ready, accounts under 90 days old, and project members, including anyone the record shows merging or closing someone else's pull request, whatever GitHub's label says. In very busy projects, replies are read from the snapshot's even random sample.
  • First pull request. The author has no earlier pull request to that project in its record of the past 365 days or in the window. Every pull request here opened in the last six months, so the look-back runs from about six months to a year. Only projects whose record isn't cut off at its cap of about 500 can tell, so most of the busiest projects are missing. The split covers 178,304 of the 592,986 pull requests read, 30%.
  • Which results use what. The waiting curve, the answered-by curve and the listed projects use first pull requests only. So do the asking figures unless a line says all outside pull requests, and the answered and merged shares beside who answers. The weekday results and who gives the first answers use all outside pull requests.
  • Who answers. The person behind the first answer to each outside pull request: who wrote the reply or review, or who merged it. Known bots are left out. A project counts once, whatever its size, so the shares beside who answers are the median project's. The comparison of similar projects uses bands of stars (under 3,000, 3,000 to 10,000, 10,000 to 30,000, 30,000 or more) and of first pull requests (under 15, 15 to 30, 30 to 60, 60 or more), and the 95% range comes from resampling projects.
  • An answer. The earliest reply or review from the project's people, a merge, or a close by them. A reply needs at least 40 characters, and automated greetings and replies from bots don't count. The clock starts when a draft is marked ready.
  • Asking. A comment from the author after the first hour, matched against a word list: ping, bump, gentle nudge, could you review, take a look, any update and similar. A hand check of 60 matches found 57 real asks. The list also misses requests: of 60 comments that only tagged someone, 25 were asking. Tool commands and CLA signatures aren't asks, but they do count as commenting. The baseline is no comment after the first hour. The inside-one-project gap compares the groups project by project, weighted by how many each has (Mantel-Haenszel), and the 95% range comes from resampling projects. We tightened the word list after a first look at the data, so this is a result to test again on the next snapshot.
  • Waiting curve. Pull requests at least four months old. One an author closed unanswered drops out from that day. The 53% in the waiting section uses all 95,078 and treats an author closing their own pull request as leaving the count, so it doesn't divide out from the curve's table.

Not covered

  • Why a project didn't answer. Silence may mean a closed list, a mailing-list workflow, or a pull request nobody wanted.
  • Not seen. Merges after the record's date, and any pull request's quality or size.
  • Cause. All of this describes what happened, including who answers and the asking and weekday comparisons. Authors who ask may also care more, or have a better pull request, and projects with shared answering may get harder ones.
  • When that person stops. We didn't measure what happens to a project when its one answerer leaves.
  • Six months is one season. Volume and answer rates may move with the time of year.
